Carborundum Universal Ltd (CUMI) — Investment Thesis

Status: BUY-AT ALERT Quality Score: 16/25 (Grade B) Classification: Quality Compounder — Overvalued at CMP Last Updated: 2026-08-01 | CMP: ₹1,064 | Entry: — | P&L: —

Recommendation: BUY AT ₹720 (0% now — standing price alert; on trigger, initiate 5% and build to a 10-12% position) Carborundum Universal is the Murugappa group's abrasives, ceramics and electrominerals maker — the domestic market leader, vertically integrated from raw grain to finished product, and the closest thing India has to a listed play on advanced ceramics for power electronics and silicon carbide. The business itself is good; the price is not. Reported FY26 profit collapsed because the company wrote off and is winding up its loss-making European and South African subsidiaries and is fighting to get cash out of its Russian plant — a self-inflicted capital-allocation wound, not a failure of the Indian core, which grew standalone profit ~29%. But even normalising for the write-offs, the stock trades near 60x earnings against a business compounding profit in the low teens at best. The one thing to watch is whether the Ceramics segment's power-electronics/SiC ramp is large enough, fast enough, to justify a growth multiple the rest of the group doesn't earn. Price ladder: Buy/Add below ₹720 · (no position ₹720–₹1,064) · Trim/Sell above ₹1,300 · Exit if Russia (VAW) triggers a fresh impairment AND Ceramics segment growth slips below 8% for two consecutive years.

Summary Verdict

Why this business?

CUMI takes cheap raw minerals — bauxite, silicon carbide grain it makes itself in electric-arc furnaces, zirconia — and turns them into things that grind, cut, insulate and line furnaces: grinding wheels, sandpaper, industrial ceramic tiles, refractory bricks, and increasingly the high-purity ceramics that sit inside electric-vehicle power modules and semiconductor equipment. It is the domestic leader across all three of its segments and one of only a handful of players worldwide that owns the whole chain from the mineral grain up to the finished abrasive or ceramic, which is where its cost advantage and its pricing power come from. The bet is not on the grinding-wheel business, which is a low-teens-margin, GDP-plus-a-bit grinder; it is on Ceramics — a 20%-plus margin segment growing double digits into power electronics, EVs, defence and semiconductors, sitting inside a company the market is currently valuing on its worst year in a decade. What I am really betting on is that the Indian core keeps compounding while the overseas mess rolls off, and that Ceramics eventually earns the group a genuine growth multiple — but only if I can buy it at a price that doesn't already assume all of that.

Strengths

  • Domestic market leadership across three integrated verticals. CUMI is the largest Indian abrasives maker and a leader in industrial ceramics and electrominerals, with FY26 consolidated revenue of ₹5,206 Cr. It makes its own silicon carbide and fused-alumina grain rather than buying it — the raw-material-to-finished-product integration is the source of its cost position.
  • Pre-existing capability — the Ceramics engine already earns 20% margins. This is the physical thing the thesis rests on and it already exists: the Ceramics segment did ₹1,268 Cr of revenue in FY26 at a segment PBIT of ₹256 Cr — a ~20% margin, four to five times the Abrasives segment's 4.3% — and grew 9.3% even in a bad year. Advanced ceramics for power electronics, metallised ceramics, and the thermal-spray/HPSiC lines are being built on top of an already-profitable, already-operating base, not from scratch.
  • Murugappa-group ownership and clean balance sheet. Promoter holding 38.9%, no pledge, and the group's governance reputation is among the best in Indian mid-caps. The company is effectively net-debt-free at the group level (borrowings ₹413 Cr against ₹199 Cr investments plus cash), self-funds its ~₹400 Cr FY27 capex, and generated ₹367 Cr operating cash flow in FY26.
  • The reported earnings understate normal earning power. FY26 consolidated PAT of ₹168 Cr (EPS ₹10.22) is depressed by ~₹134 Cr of exceptional write-offs tied to shutting down overseas units. The Indian standalone business — the real engine — grew PAT ~29% to ₹416 Cr and held a 16% operating margin.

Concerns

  • The overseas write-offs are a capital-allocation failure, not bad luck. CUMI chose to buy Volzhsky Abrasive Works in Russia, Foskor Zirconia in South Africa, and AWUKO in Germany. It is now voluntarily winding up the German and South African units and has ~₹297 Cr of cash trapped inside the Russian subsidiary under sanctions-related repatriation restrictions, with the ultimate recoverability of those assets still being assessed. This is management deploying shareholder capital abroad and destroying it — which matters because it makes the "just add back the one-time charge" normalisation optimistic: if they keep doing value-destructive M&A, the add-back recurs.
  • The weakness is partly operational, not purely a one-timer. Group segment PBIT fell from ₹615 Cr in FY25 to ₹444 Cr in FY26 — a 28% drop that has nothing to do with the exceptional line. Electrominerals PBIT halved (₹177 Cr → ₹91 Cr) on energy and raw-material cost inflation and the Russian drag; Abrasives PBIT fell from ₹151 Cr to ₹97 Cr. The core is not immune; two of three segments saw real margin compression.
  • The price already assumes the recovery. At ₹1,064 the stock is ~78x reported earnings and ~60x my normalised estimate — above the higher-quality, debt-free, 21%-ROCE peer Grindwell Norton at ~54x. Group ROCE is 10.5% and ROE 6.9% (both depressed, but even normalised ROCE is only ~14%). You are paying a growth multiple for a group whose consolidated profit has actually shrunk over three and five years.

How does this actually compound? (one-line summary; full breakdown in Section 3)

Growth comes from the Indian core reinvesting operating cash into higher-margin Ceramics capacity — advanced ceramics for power electronics, refractories, thermal-spray powders — at incremental returns meaningfully above the group's blended ~14% normalised ROCE, so the mix keeps shifting toward the 20%-margin segment. The headline group ROIC understates this because it is dragged down by low-return Abrasives and value-destroyed overseas capital; the real engine is Ceramics (see Section 3).

What does the market think — and where do I disagree?

At ₹1,064 on my ~₹17 normalised EPS, the market is paying ~60x and, by the reverse-DCF in Section 5.2, is implicitly underwriting roughly 16-17% earnings growth sustained for the better part of a decade. The Indian standalone business can plausibly compound low-to-mid teens, and Ceramics faster than that — but the group is more than Ceramics, and Abrasives and Electrominerals together are ~75% of revenue growing mid-single-digits at compressed margins. My estimate for durable group EPS growth is ~12-13%, roughly 4 percentage points below what the price assumes. The gap is not that the business is bad; it is that the market is valuing the whole group on the Ceramics segment's trajectory while paying for the whole group's asset base.

A. Why reported profit collapsed while the Indian business thrived — reading standalone vs consolidated

This is the single most important thing to get right on CUMI, because the search-engine headlines contradict each other: one set says "PAT rises 29% to ₹416 Cr," another says "PAT falls 33.5%." Both are true, and they describe different entities.

The standalone entity is CUMI's Indian operations only. In FY26 it did ₹3,063 Cr of revenue at a 16% operating margin and grew net profit ~29% to ₹416 Cr (EPS ₹21.85). That figure also includes dividend income the parent receives from its subsidiaries, which is why it can look strong even when some subsidiaries are struggling.

The consolidated entity adds every subsidiary — the Indian ones plus Russia (Volzhsky), South Africa (Foskor Zirconia), Germany (AWUKO), and others. In FY26 consolidated revenue was ₹5,206 Cr but consolidated PAT was only ₹168 Cr (EPS ₹10.22), because the overseas subsidiaries lost money operationally and the company booked ~₹134 Cr of exceptional charges to shut two of them down.

So the group's own healthy Indian profit of ₹416 Cr got more than halved on the way to a ₹168 Cr consolidated number by overseas losses and write-offs. That is the whole story. For valuation you must use consolidated earnings — using the ₹416 Cr standalone figure would double-count, because that number already contains dividends paid up from the very subsidiaries whose losses you'd then be ignoring. My normalised base (~₹17 EPS, ~₹300-320 Cr consolidated PAT) is the consolidated ₹168 Cr with the ~₹134 Cr one-time charge added back — and even that is a judgement, because part of the overseas drag was operational, not exceptional, and part of the going-forward benefit is that the loss-making units are simply gone.

1. Business Summary

Carborundum Universal (CUMI), founded 1954 and part of the Murugappa group, is India's leading manufacturer of abrasives (grinding wheels, coated abrasives, super abrasives), industrial ceramics and refractories, and electrominerals (silicon carbide and fused-alumina grain). It sells to virtually every manufacturing sector — auto, engineering, steel, construction, electronics — both in India and globally, and is vertically integrated from mineral grain up to finished product. FY26 was a trough year: consolidated revenue crossed ₹5,000 Cr but reported profit collapsed on ~₹134 Cr of write-offs tied to winding up its German and South African subsidiaries and the sanctions-hit Russian plant, even as the Indian standalone business grew profit ~29%.

6. Competitive Landscape

Market Position

CUMI is the largest Indian abrasives manufacturer and a domestic leader in industrial ceramics, refractories and electrominerals, competing against Grindwell Norton (Saint-Gobain group) and Wendt India (in which CUMI itself holds a stake) domestically, and against global specialty players (3M, Saint-Gobain, Tyrolit, CoorsTek, Kyocera) and low-cost Chinese producers in electrominerals and engineered ceramics. Glossary — Key Industry Terms

🟢 KEEP — reviewer: Keep for genuinely niche terms (silicon carbide, electrominerals). Watch the drift into re-defining things already explained inline.

Abrasives Products that grind, cut, polish or finish other materials — bonded abrasives (grinding wheels), coated abrasives (sandpaper), and super abrasives (diamond/CBN wheels). CUMI's largest revenue segment but its lowest-margin (~4%), because much of it is commodity-like and sold on price against Grindwell Norton and imports.

Ceramics (industrial/advanced ceramics) Engineered ceramic components — wear-resistant tiles/liners, metallised ceramics, and advanced ceramics for power electronics — made by sintering (fusing ceramic powder at very high temperature). Because they are specified to customer designs and hard to qualify, they command ~20% segment margins. This is CUMI's profit engine and the home of its growth optionality.

Electrominerals The raw grain business: silicon carbide (SiC) and fused alumina made in energy-intensive electric-arc furnaces. CUMI uses this captively (feeding its own abrasives and ceramics) and sells it externally. Energy-cost-sensitive and exposed to Chinese oversupply — which is why its FY26 PBIT halved. The Russian VAW plant is an electrominerals asset.

Silicon Carbide (SiC) / High-Purity SiC (HPSiC) A very hard, heat- and voltage-tolerant compound. As an abrasive grain it grinds hard metals; in high-purity form it is the basis for next-generation power semiconductors used in EV inverters and renewable-energy systems, which switch more efficiently than silicon. CUMI making its own SiC grain is the physical basis of its integration advantage and its power-electronics ambition.

Volzhsky Abrasive Works (VAW) CUMI's Russian silicon-carbide/electrominerals subsidiary. The war-related sanctions environment has trapped ~₹297 Cr of its cash (can't be repatriated to India) and clouds the recoverability of its assets — the single biggest capital risk on CUMI's balance sheet.

Foskor Zirconia / CUMI AWUKO Overseas subsidiaries — a zirconia (a ceramic mineral) unit in South Africa and an abrasives unit in Germany — that were chronically loss-making and are being voluntarily wound up, driving most of FY26's ~₹134 Cr exceptional charge.

Refractories Heat-resistant materials (bricks, linings, castables) that line furnaces and kilns in steel, cement and glass making. Part of CUMI's Ceramics segment; the FY27 capex includes a new refractory tunnel kiln.

PBIT (Profit Before Interest and Tax) Segment-level operating profit before financing and tax. Used here to compare how profitable each of CUMI's three segments is — the ~20% Ceramics PBIT margin vs ~4% Abrasives is the clearest single picture of where the company actually makes money.

Standalone vs Consolidated Standalone = the parent Indian company only (and includes dividends it receives from subsidiaries). Consolidated = the parent plus all subsidiaries combined. For CUMI in FY26 the two diverge sharply (₹416 Cr standalone PAT vs ₹168 Cr consolidated) because the overseas subsidiaries lost money — consolidated is the correct valuation base.
