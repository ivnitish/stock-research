### Why this business?

Symbiotec grows microorganisms in steel tanks and runs the output through multi-step chemistry to make the raw drug substance for steroids and hormones: hydrocortisone, testosterone, methylprednisolone. The market it serves is small, and inside it Symbiotec is dominant. By volume it holds 80.1% of the global hydrocortisone API market, 76.4% of testosterone and 76.0% of methylprednisolone (F&S Report, commissioned by the company, so treat the precision with care while accepting the direction). Customers cannot switch easily, because a formulator that changes API supplier must redo validation and bioequivalence work and refile with the regulator. That is why the top five customers have been with Symbiotec more than ten years on average.

That business earns 30.4% on the capital employed in it, and it produced the entire ₹869 Cr of FY26 revenue and ₹110 Cr of profit.

Over the last two years the company built a second business alongside it — a 400 KL fermentation plant at Ujjain for contract manufacturing, and a 20-million-vial injectables plant at Mhow. Together they cost ₹871 Cr, they are commissioned, and in FY26 they generated ₹34 Cr of revenue and lost ₹28.5 Cr. The IPO asks you to pay 57.8x the earnings of the first business for the combination of both.

### Strengths

Global volume leadership in three molecules above 76% share, held with 43 US FDA drug master files and 23 European certificates of suitability, against a customer base of 200+ across 40 countries with more than ten years of average tenure at the top of it. Churn in this category is structurally low because switching costs are regulatory, not commercial.

Backward integration into the fermentation of its own key intermediates, which is the reason the core business runs a 26.6% EBITDA margin rather than the mid-teens a converter would earn. Dependence on Chinese intermediates fell from 52.79% of total expenses in FY24 to 23.88% in FY26 — real progress, though it rose from 16.82% in FY25, so the direction is not monotonic.

The base business alone earns 30.43% adjusted ROCE (FY26), up from 27.71% and 24.59% in the two prior years. The company discloses this figure itself, excluding the two subsidiaries that hold the new plants. It is the single most favourable number in the prospectus and it is rising.

Balance sheet is not stretched: net debt ₹380.9 Cr at 1.64x EBITDA, interest cover 7.1x, related-party transactions 1.59% of revenue, contingent liabilities nil under Ind AS 37, and a clean audit from S R B C & Co (an EY member firm).

### Concerns

**The capital is spent and the returns are not there.** Gross fixed assets went from ₹630 Cr to ₹1,364 Cr in FY26 alone (RHP note 2a). Revenue on that block is 0.64x. Over FY24–FY26, capital employed rose ₹561 Cr and EBIT rose ₹40 Cr — an incremental pre-tax return of 7.2%, about 5.2% after tax, against the 20%+ this framework requires. Consolidated ROCE has fallen every year through the build: 14.03% → 11.80% → 11.56%. None of this is hidden; the RHP's own "Adjusted ROCE" line exists precisely to strip it out. The question is whether an IPO buyer should pay 57.8x for the adjusted number while owning the unadjusted one.

**FY27 earnings are more likely to fall than rise.** The new plants were capitalised part-way through FY26, so only a partial year of depreciation has hit. Running the FY26 rates from note 2a across the full closing gross block adds roughly ₹18 Cr of depreciation in FY27 before a rupee of new capex — ₹14.1 Cr on plant and machinery, ₹2.1 Cr on buildings, ₹2.0 Cr on electricals. Finance costs already rose from ₹16.0 Cr to ₹25.3 Cr as interest stopped being capitalised into construction. The ₹112.5 Cr debt repayment from the fresh issue saves around ₹9–10 Cr of that. Net, roughly ₹13 Cr of after-tax drag on a ₹110 Cr base, against CDMO revenue that the company itself expects to begin only in late FY27.

**The contracts are described, never quantified.** The RHP names "a five-year insulin drug substance agreement, a GLP-1 manufacturing agreement, and a ten-year contract for classical fermentation-based APIs." No counterparty, no volume, no value, no minimum offtake. "Take or pay" appears once in the entire 551-page document, in a risk factor listing generic restrictive covenants, and never in any description of the CDMO book. Phase 0.5 of this framework allows a built-but-underutilised asset to count as pre-existing capability only when unit economics are visible from comparable operations. Agreements that are described but never sized do not clear that test, which leaves ₹871 Cr of the asset base venture-grade.

**The base plant is not full, and its volume shrank.** Pithampur fermentation ran at 72.91% of effective capacity in FY26, down from 82.52%; actual production fell from 247.57 KL to 218.75 KL. FY26's 15.65% revenue growth came from price and mix, not volume, and geographically from the US (₹30.2 Cr → ₹114.0 Cr) while India fell from ₹336.8 Cr to ₹286.5 Cr. Adding 400 KL on top of a 300 KL base running at 73% and declining is a bet on demand that has not yet appeared in the base plant.

**A live, unresolved FDA matter at the main plant, nine days before the issue opens.** The US FDA concluded an inspection at Pithampur on 14 August 2026 and issued four observations; the company's response was still due when the RHP was filed on 18 August. Rau also received a Form 483 following its 2026 inspection. Pithampur carries 492.67 MT of chemical synthesis and all 300 KL of producing fermentation, and the US is now 13.1% of revenue and was the whole of FY26's growth. Disclosed properly — but the outcome is unknown and the timing means no investor can price it.

**Three transactions that set the tone of the issue.** In December 2025 the promoters subscribed a rights issue at ₹276. On 17 August 2026 the two private-equity holders transferred 1,712,344 shares to Satwani Holdings LLP at ₹200 each — ₹34.2 Cr paid for stock being offered to the public seven days later at up to ₹988, worth ₹169.2 Cr at that price. The RHP explains this as an "upside sharing arrangement" under the Investment Agreement, which is a contractual promoter incentive and not improper. Satwani Holdings is then selling ₹144 Cr in the OFS, about 1,457,490 shares at the cap price, near-identical to the 1,712,344 it received at ₹200. Separately, Motilal Oswal Investment Advisors is a book-running lead manager while its affiliate India Business Excellence Fund–III is selling ₹475 Cr. Each item is disclosed and none is improper, but the alignment they describe points away from the incoming shareholder.

**91.5% of the money goes to existing holders.** Fresh issue ₹150 Cr of a ₹1,757 Cr offer. Rosewood takes out ₹988 Cr against a ₹147.21 cost, IBEF-III ₹475 Cr against the same, the promoter ₹144 Cr against ₹49.43. The company receives ₹150 Cr, of which ₹112.5 Cr repays debt. Promoter and promoter group hold 36.41% pre-offer — below the 40% this framework flags — and less after.

**The one prior venture into something new was written off.** Propel Pharma Corp, a Delaware R&D subsidiary, was dissolved in June 2025 because, in the RHP's words, "the projects on which the entity was engaged were not eventually commercialized." Receivables of ₹20.5 Cr and ₹19.3 Cr were written off in FY26 and FY25, and the company booked a ₹52.8 Cr standalone impairment against Navisci Pte Ltd. That is the available track record for management deploying capital outside the core steroid business, and it is the same question the CDMO and injectables spending raises.

**Cash has not come out.** Free cash flow was negative in each of the last three years — −₹22 Cr, −₹261 Cr, −₹51 Cr — cumulatively −₹334 Cr. Operating cash flow covered just 0.23x of EBITDA in FY25. Inventory sits at 274 days of cost of goods, which long fermentation cycles partly explain but do not fully excuse. Against the company, ₹150.8 Cr of tax and civil claims are outstanding while contingent liabilities are reported nil.

### The Compounding Equation

The engine that exists works like this. A formulator making a hydrocortisone injection needs the drug substance to be identical batch after batch, because a change means new stability data and a regulatory refiling that costs more than the API does. Symbiotec ferments its own precursor from soy sterols instead of buying it from China, so it controls both the cost and the consistency. That is why it holds 80% of world volume in the molecule and why 26.6% of every rupee of revenue reaches EBITDA. Capital employed in that business turns at 30.4% pre-tax. If the company reinvested all of it at that rate, intrinsic value would compound around 30% a year.

The engine being sold is different. Of the ₹1,364 Cr of gross fixed assets, ₹871 Cr sits at Ujjain and Mhow and earns nothing yet. Blended, the company returns 11.56% on capital and falling. Whether the second engine ever matches the first depends on contracts whose size the prospectus does not disclose.

### What does the market think — and where do I disagree?

At ₹988 the post-issue market capitalisation is ₹6,348 Cr against FY26 profit of ₹110 Cr. Strip the two loss-making subsidiaries and the underlying business earned ₹138.4 Cr. Put a generous 30x on that — appropriate for a 30% ROCE franchise growing low teens — and the core is worth ₹4,152 Cr. The remaining ₹2,196 Cr is what the offer prices the new capacity at, against ₹871 Cr of construction cost. That is 2.5x the money spent, on plants that produced ₹34 Cr of revenue and ₹28.5 Cr of losses last year.

For that ₹2,196 Cr to be fair, the new assets must eventually earn ₹73 Cr of profit, which at the company's 12.6% net margin means ₹580 Cr of revenue — an asset turn of 0.67x on the ₹871 Cr. The company suggests 1.5–2x is achievable, which would imply ₹1,300–1,740 Cr and make today's price cheap.

So the disagreement is narrow and specific. If the fermentation contracts are real and large, the price is defensible. The prospectus had 551 pages in which to say how large, and did not. Until it does, paying 2.5x cost for idle assets and 57.8x trailing earnings for the whole means paying in advance for an outcome the prospectus declines to size.

## Detailed Analysis

### A. Deep Dive — the ₹871 Cr question

This note turns on ₹871 Cr of plant and whether it ever earns anything.

The RHP gives the gross block by facility as of 31 March 2026: Rau ₹172.8 Cr, Pithampur ₹470.5 Cr, Ujjain ₹479.5 Cr, Mhow ₹391.9 Cr. Rau and Pithampur — ₹643.3 Cr — produced the entire ₹869.1 Cr of FY26 revenue. Ujjain and Mhow — ₹871.4 Cr, more than half the total asset base — produced ₹34.0 Cr, being ₹33.05 Cr of injectables and ₹1.09 Cr of CDMO.

The subsidiary results make the drag explicit. Symbiotec Zenfold Private Limited, which holds Ujjain, lost ₹26.86 Cr in FY26 against a ₹2.68 Cr profit in FY24. Knovea Pharmaceutical Private Limited, which holds Mhow, lost ₹1.64 Cr after ₹13.81 Cr and ₹10.99 Cr in the two prior years. The parent has lent them ₹396.0 Cr and ₹574.0 Cr respectively and guaranteed a further ₹21.2 Cr.

The company's disclosure is straightforward about all this — the "Adjusted ROCE" of 30.43% is defined in the KPI notes as excluding exactly these two entities "because they are not fully commercialised yet." That is a fair presentation of the core business. It is also, read the other way, a precise measurement of how much of the asset base is currently earning nothing.

One reconciliation note. The RHP reports a gross fixed asset turnover of 1.54x for FY26. Note 2a puts closing gross PPE at ₹1,363.75 Cr, which against ₹869.15 Cr of revenue gives 0.64x. The stated ratio appears to be computed on a standalone or pre-capitalisation base; the consolidated figure an equity buyer owns is 0.64x. Flagging the gap rather than guessing at the reconciliation.

### 3. Compounding Engine — Five Whys on the ROIC

*Why does the core business earn 30% on capital?* Because it converts a cheap agricultural input into a product customers cannot easily buy elsewhere.

*Why can't they buy it elsewhere?* Because roughly three-quarters of world volume in these molecules runs through Symbiotec, and the alternative suppliers are Chinese fermentation houses or higher-cost Western plants.

*Why is Symbiotec the low-cost producer?* Because it ferments its own steroid precursors from soy sterols rather than importing intermediates, which removed the largest input cost and the largest supply risk simultaneously.

*Why has nobody replicated that?* Because industrial-scale steroid fermentation requires proprietary microbial strains, multi-step chemistry validated to over 400 synthesis steps under cGMP, and a plant that takes years and hundreds of crores to build and qualify.

*Why does that persist?* Because the binding constraint is regulatory, not industrial. A competitor could build the plant. It could not make a formulator refile a product with the FDA to use it, absent a price gap large enough to justify the switching cost — and Symbiotec's own cost position ensures no such gap opens.

The fifth answer points at something structural rather than something the company controls contractually, which is the durable outcome the test looks for. The moat is real, and it is narrow. It protects three molecules in a category growing at single digits, and none of it extends to the CDMO, insulin or injectables businesses the IPO is priced on. Those compete on price and capacity against Divi's, Laurus and Cohance, where Symbiotec has no share, no track record and, on the evidence of the prospectus, no disclosed contract size.

### 4. World-State Under the Base Case (Phase 4.5.2)

**Two years out (FY28).** Revenue around ₹1,250 Cr with CDMO contributing its first meaningful ₹100–150 Cr, EBITDA margin compressed to roughly 25% as full-year depreciation on ₹871 Cr lands ahead of full utilisation, PAT around ₹150 Cr. Management's agenda is qualifying a fermentation plant for contract customers who audit differently from API customers, and doing it while the base plant sits at 73%. They have never run a CDMO business; the prospectus reports ₹1.09 Cr of CDMO revenue in its best year.

**Five years out (FY31).** Revenue ₹2,200–2,600 Cr if the asset turn the company suggests materialises, EBITDA back above 27%, PAT ₹350–420 Cr. The management challenge shifts to holding steroid share while running three unrelated businesses, and to the succession question inside a family holding a diminished stake.

**What success creates.** A profitable Indian fermentation CDMO at scale attracts Divi's and Laurus, both of whom have deeper balance sheets and existing CDMO relationships. Success in GLP-1 attracts every Indian API house at once. And a company that lists at 57.8x and then delivers has no multiple expansion left to give, so the entire return has to come from earnings growth.

### 5. Margin Behaviour Through Stress (Phase 4.5.3)

Margins here are in state 3 — flat-to-compressed during a heavy capex phase. EBITDA margin ran 24.48% → 27.26% → 26.59%.

The framework requires one specific verification before accepting capex absorption as benign: the prior capex cycle must have converted at ROIC above 20%. Symbiotec passes on the *core* — Rau and Pithampur return 30.43% adjusted, and that number has risen for three years. It fails on the *current* cycle, where FY24–FY26 incremental capital of ₹561 Cr produced ₹40 Cr of incremental EBIT.

The distinction matters. The prior-cycle proof exists for steroid API capacity, which is what the family has built and run for two decades. It does not exist for fermentation CDMO, biologics or injectables, which is where ₹871 Cr just went and where the only comparable data point — Propel Pharma — was written off.

### 6. Market Label vs Reality (Phase 4.5.4)

**Market label:** a scarce global leader in a complex API niche, with GLP-1 and CDMO optionality, worth a premium multiple.

**Our label:** an excellent, slow-growing, 30%-ROCE steroid API franchise, correctly identified — bundled with ₹871 Cr of unproven capacity and sold at a price that assumes the second part already works.

**Label-change catalyst:** the first two quarterly results after listing. Either Ujjain reports commercial revenue with named contracts, or it does not. Consensus revises on that print, in one direction or the other, and the ₹418 / ₹555 levels above are set to be ready for it.

**The P/S band conflicts, and here is the reconciliation.** Phase 5.4 assigns a 25–30% EBITDA margin business a fair P/S of 8–10x. At 7.3x, Symbiotec sits below that band and screens cheap. The band should be overruled here. It was built on the assumption that a 26% EBITDA margin implies decent returns on capital, which is true when revenue turns the asset base at 1.5x or better. Symbiotec turns its gross block 0.64x. A rupee of revenue at 26% margin is worth less when it takes ₹1.57 of gross fixed assets to produce than when it takes ₹0.67. P/E and EV/EBITDA account for that capital. P/S does not, which is why it misprices this company.

**Incremental ROIC (Phase 1.1), full arithmetic.**

```
Capital employed = total equity + borrowings + lease liabilities + DTL − DTA

FY26  11,495.90 + 3,879.14 + 30.76 + 58.13   = ₹15,463.93 mn  (₹1,546.4 Cr)
FY24   7,148.44 + 2,472.07 + 33.91 + 199.44  = ₹ 9,853.86 mn  (₹  985.4 Cr)
ΔCE                                          = ₹   561.0 Cr

EBIT = PBT + finance costs
FY26  1,533.63 + 253.27 = ₹1,786.90 mn  (₹178.69 Cr)
FY24  1,309.83 +  72.36 = ₹1,382.19 mn  (₹138.22 Cr)
ΔEBIT                   = ₹ 40.47 Cr

Incremental pre-tax return = 40.47 / 561.0 = 7.2%
Incremental ROIC (28.3% tax) = 5.2%
```

## Research Log

**2026-08-23 — Initial note, written from the RHP.**

Source of record is the Red Herring Prospectus filed 18 August 2026 (551 pages, Khaitan & Co), downloaded and text-extracted in full. Everything numeric above traces to it: capacity utilisation (p. 243–244), customer concentration (risk factor 5), WACA and pricing history (Basis for Offer Price VIII), peer KPIs (p. 155–159), restated consolidated accounts (p. 309 onward), note 2a for gross block, litigation summary (p. 56), subsidiary results (risk factor 57–58).

The starting input was a BusinessLine IPO Watch piece by Sai Prabhakar Yadavalli (company website PDF, 3 pages), which recommends subscribing on a three-to-five-year view. Where the RHP and the article diverge, the RHP wins. Three specific divergences worth recording:

The article states the CDMO capacity is covered by "take or pay contracts for long periods of 5-10 years for a significant part." The phrase "take or pay" occurs once in the RHP, inside a generic list of restrictive covenants in business agreements, and never in connection with the CDMO book. The agreements are named but never sized. This changed the Phase 0.5 conclusion from form 2 (built but underutilised, unit economics visible) to venture-grade, and it is the largest single driver of the Grade C.

The article says backward integration is "replacing Chinese imports" at 80% of the portfolio. Chinese raw material was 23.88% of total expenses in FY26 against 16.82% in FY25 — the three-year direction from 52.79% is genuinely good, the one-year direction is not.

The article says the ₹480 Cr / ₹130 Cr / ₹400 Cr facilities "should generate revenue in line with current asset turnover ratio of 1.5-2 times." The RHP's own gross fixed asset turnover is stated at 1.54x, but note 2a gives closing gross PPE of ₹1,363.75 Cr against ₹869.15 Cr of revenue, which is 0.64x. The 1.5–2x assumption is doing heavy lifting in the article's implied upside and does not reconcile with the consolidated balance sheet. Also, the ₹130 Cr insulin unit is described in the RHP as a 14 KL facility the company "is expanding" at Ujjain, not as completed capacity.

Two things the article does not cover at all, both material: customer and product concentration (top 10 products at 78.96% of revenue), and the 14 August FDA inspection at Pithampur.

Grey-market premium was checked and deliberately excluded from the valuation. It is an unregulated informal quote and carries no information about business value.

**Not verified, and what would help.** The 38.2% corticosteroid and 23.8% steroidal-hormone global share figures come from the F&S Report commissioned by the issuer and could not be independently corroborated; the same applies to the 80.1% / 76.4% / 76.0% molecule-level shares. The counterparties to the five-year insulin, GLP-1 and ten-year fermentation agreements are not disclosed anywhere in the RHP. The identity of Customer 1 (16.13% of sales) is withheld for want of consent. The composition of the ₹150.8 Cr of claims against the company was not broken out to the individual matter level. The single most valuable disclosure for re-underwriting this would be a quarterly result naming a CDMO counterparty with contracted volume and tenure.
