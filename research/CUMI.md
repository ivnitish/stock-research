## Summary Verdict

### Why this business?

CUMI takes cheap raw minerals — bauxite, silicon carbide grain it makes itself in electric-arc furnaces, zirconia — and turns them into things that grind, cut, insulate and line furnaces: grinding wheels, sandpaper, industrial ceramic tiles, refractory bricks, and increasingly the high-purity ceramics that sit inside electric-vehicle power modules and semiconductor equipment. It is the domestic leader across all three of its segments and one of only a handful of players worldwide that owns the whole chain from the mineral grain up to the finished abrasive or ceramic, which is where its cost advantage and its pricing power come from. The bet is not on the grinding-wheel business, which is a low-teens-margin, GDP-plus-a-bit grinder; it is on Ceramics — a 20%-plus margin segment growing double digits into power electronics, EVs, defence and semiconductors, sitting inside a company the market is currently valuing on its worst year in a decade. What I am really betting on is that the Indian core keeps compounding while the overseas mess rolls off, and that Ceramics eventually earns the group a genuine growth multiple — but only if I can buy it at a price that doesn't already assume all of that.

### Strengths

- **Domestic market leadership across three integrated verticals.** CUMI is the largest Indian abrasives maker and a leader in industrial ceramics and electrominerals, with FY26 consolidated revenue of ₹5,206 Cr. It makes its own silicon carbide and fused-alumina grain rather than buying it — the raw-material-to-finished-product integration is the source of its cost position.
- **Pre-existing capability — the Ceramics engine already earns 20% margins.** This is the physical thing the thesis rests on and it already exists: the Ceramics segment did ₹1,268 Cr of revenue in FY26 at a segment PBIT of ₹256 Cr — a ~20% margin, four to five times the Abrasives segment's 4.3% — and grew 9.3% even in a bad year. Advanced ceramics for power electronics, metallised ceramics, and the thermal-spray/HPSiC lines are being built on top of an already-profitable, already-operating base, not from scratch.
- **Murugappa-group ownership and clean balance sheet.** Promoter holding 38.9%, no pledge, and the group's governance reputation is among the best in Indian mid-caps. The company is effectively net-debt-free at the group level (borrowings ₹413 Cr against ₹199 Cr investments plus cash), self-funds its ~₹400 Cr FY27 capex, and generated ₹367 Cr operating cash flow in FY26.
- **The reported earnings understate normal earning power.** FY26 consolidated PAT of ₹168 Cr (EPS ₹10.22) is depressed by ~₹134 Cr of exceptional write-offs tied to shutting down overseas units. The Indian standalone business — the real engine — grew PAT ~29% to ₹416 Cr and held a 16% operating margin.

### Concerns

- **The overseas write-offs are a capital-allocation failure, not bad luck.** CUMI chose to buy Volzhsky Abrasive Works in Russia, Foskor Zirconia in South Africa, and AWUKO in Germany. It is now voluntarily winding up the German and South African units and has ~₹297 Cr of cash trapped inside the Russian subsidiary under sanctions-related repatriation restrictions, with the ultimate recoverability of those assets still being assessed. This is management deploying shareholder capital abroad and destroying it — which matters because it makes the "just add back the one-time charge" normalisation optimistic: if they keep doing value-destructive M&A, the add-back recurs.
- **The weakness is partly operational, not purely a one-timer.** Group segment PBIT fell from ₹615 Cr in FY25 to ₹444 Cr in FY26 — a 28% drop that has nothing to do with the exceptional line. Electrominerals PBIT halved (₹177 Cr → ₹91 Cr) on energy and raw-material cost inflation and the Russian drag; Abrasives PBIT fell from ₹151 Cr to ₹97 Cr. The core is not immune; two of three segments saw real margin compression.
- **The price already assumes the recovery.** At ₹1,064 the stock is ~78x reported earnings and ~60x my normalised estimate — above the higher-quality, debt-free, 21%-ROCE peer Grindwell Norton at ~54x. Group ROCE is 10.5% and ROE 6.9% (both depressed, but even normalised ROCE is only ~14%). You are paying a growth multiple for a group whose consolidated profit has actually shrunk over three and five years.

### How does this actually compound? (one-line summary; full breakdown in Section 3)

Growth comes from the Indian core reinvesting operating cash into higher-margin Ceramics capacity — advanced ceramics for power electronics, refractories, thermal-spray powders — at incremental returns meaningfully above the group's blended ~14% normalised ROCE, so the mix keeps shifting toward the 20%-margin segment. The headline group ROIC understates this because it is dragged down by low-return Abrasives and value-destroyed overseas capital; the real engine is Ceramics (see Section 3).

### What does the market think — and where do I disagree?

At ₹1,064 on my ~₹17 normalised EPS, the market is paying ~60x and, by the reverse-DCF in Section 5.2, is implicitly underwriting roughly 16-17% earnings growth sustained for the better part of a decade. The Indian standalone business can plausibly compound low-to-mid teens, and Ceramics faster than that — but the group is more than Ceramics, and Abrasives and Electrominerals together are ~75% of revenue growing mid-single-digits at compressed margins. My estimate for durable group EPS growth is ~12-13%, roughly 4 percentage points below what the price assumes. The gap is not that the business is bad; it is that the market is valuing the whole group on the Ceramics segment's trajectory while paying for the whole group's asset base.

### Second-Order Stress Test

**5-Whys on the ROIC engine:**

> Why does this business earn its returns (and why are they only middling at the group level today)?
> 1. Because Ceramics earns ~20% segment margins while Abrasives earns ~4% and Electrominerals ~6% — the blend is mediocre.
> 2. Why is Ceramics so much better? → Because it makes specialised, engineered products (metallised ceramics, wear components, advanced ceramics for power electronics) where the customer specifies to CUMI's design and can't easily switch, versus commodity grinding wheels sold on price.
> 3. Why can't competitors just make the same specialised ceramics? → Because it requires decades of accumulated process know-how in high-temperature sintering plus qualification into customer supply chains that takes years, and CUMI makes its own silicon-carbide grain, so it controls quality from the raw material up.
> 4. Why does owning the grain matter? → Because silicon carbide is made in energy-intensive electric-arc furnaces that are hard to permit and build; captive grain gives cost and supply security a trader-buyer can't match.
> 5. Why is that durable? → Because the barrier is structural — furnace assets, power access, process IP, and multi-year customer qualifications — not a contract or a person. That part is a real moat. But it lives mostly in Ceramics and captive Electrominerals; the Abrasives half of the company competes on price against Grindwell and imports, and there the fifth answer is "not much." The group moat is real but narrow.

**The world in 2 years if base case plays out:**

By FY28 the overseas noise is gone from the P&L — Foskor and AWUKO wound up, the Russian unit either ring-fenced or written down to nil — so reported consolidated PAT snaps back to roughly the ₹300-350 Cr region (EPS ~₹16-18) purely on the absence of write-offs and some margin normalisation. Revenue is ~₹5,700-6,000 Cr with Ceramics approaching 27-28% of the mix. Management's agenda is executing the ₹400 Cr/year capex into advanced ceramics for power electronics, an integrated thermal-spray furnace, and a refractory tunnel kiln — and, harder than anything they've done before, actually converting the "HPSiC / power-electronics ceramics" narrative into disclosed, material revenue rather than a slide in the investor deck.

**What success itself triggers:**

If Ceramics scales into power electronics as hoped, CUMI attracts direct competition from global specialty-ceramics majors (CoorsTek, Kyocera, 3M) and from Chinese SiC capacity that has been flooding the electrominerals market — the same competition that is already pressuring Electrominerals margins. And a genuine re-rating to a 45x+ "power-electronics ceramics" multiple would leave the stock priced for a segment that is still only a quarter of revenue, so the valuation risk compounds even if the operations deliver.

## Deep Dives

### A. Why reported profit collapsed while the Indian business thrived — reading standalone vs consolidated

This is the single most important thing to get right on CUMI, because the search-engine headlines contradict each other: one set says "PAT rises 29% to ₹416 Cr," another says "PAT falls 33.5%." Both are true, and they describe different entities.

The **standalone** entity is CUMI's Indian operations only. In FY26 it did ₹3,063 Cr of revenue at a 16% operating margin and grew net profit ~29% to ₹416 Cr (EPS ₹21.85). That figure also *includes dividend income the parent receives from its subsidiaries*, which is why it can look strong even when some subsidiaries are struggling.

The **consolidated** entity adds every subsidiary — the Indian ones plus Russia (Volzhsky), South Africa (Foskor Zirconia), Germany (AWUKO), and others. In FY26 consolidated revenue was ₹5,206 Cr but consolidated PAT was only ₹168 Cr (EPS ₹10.22), because the overseas subsidiaries lost money operationally *and* the company booked ~₹134 Cr of exceptional charges to shut two of them down.

So the group's own healthy Indian profit of ₹416 Cr got more than halved on the way to a ₹168 Cr consolidated number by overseas losses and write-offs. That is the whole story. For valuation you must use **consolidated** earnings — using the ₹416 Cr standalone figure would double-count, because that number already contains dividends paid up from the very subsidiaries whose losses you'd then be ignoring. My normalised base (~₹17 EPS, ~₹300-320 Cr consolidated PAT) is the consolidated ₹168 Cr with the ~₹134 Cr one-time charge added back — and even that is a *judgement*, because part of the overseas drag was operational, not exceptional, and part of the going-forward benefit is that the loss-making units are simply gone.

Read this table and the thesis writes itself. **Ceramics is the crown jewel** — a fifth of revenue but well over half the group's segment profit, at a 20% margin that is ~5x the Abrasives margin. It is also the fastest grower and the home of the power-electronics/advanced-ceramics optionality. **Abrasives** is the biggest revenue line but earns almost nothing (4.3%) — it is the commodity grinding-wheel business competing with Grindwell and imports. **Electrominerals** (which includes the captive silicon-carbide grain and the Russian VAW plant) saw PBIT halve on energy costs and the Russia drag.

This is why the compounding is real but the *pace* is low-teens, not the 20%+ that would justify the current multiple.

## 1. Business Summary

Carborundum Universal (CUMI), founded 1954 and part of the Murugappa group, is India's leading manufacturer of abrasives (grinding wheels, coated abrasives, super abrasives), industrial ceramics and refractories, and electrominerals (silicon carbide and fused-alumina grain). It sells to virtually every manufacturing sector — auto, engineering, steel, construction, electronics — both in India and globally, and is vertically integrated from mineral grain up to finished product. FY26 was a trough year: consolidated revenue crossed ₹5,000 Cr but reported profit collapsed on ~₹134 Cr of write-offs tied to winding up its German and South African subsidiaries and the sanctions-hit Russian plant, even as the Indian standalone business grew profit ~29%.

## 3. Compounding Engine — Q&A

### Q0. Operating Leverage Test — does growth create margin leverage?

- Revenue growth (FY26 vs 3yr avg): consolidated ~6.5% YoY; 3-year revenue CAGR ~4%.
- EBITDA/operating margin now vs 3 years ago: ~11% (FY26) vs ~14-16% (FY23-24) — **compressed, not expanding.**
- **Operating leverage present?** Not currently — margins fell while revenue grew, the opposite of leverage. The leverage is *latent*: a chunk of the FY26 compression is the overseas drag and energy costs, which should reverse, at which point the fixed-cost base is spread over recovering volume.
- Capacity utilisation: not cleanly disclosed; the ₹400 Cr FY27 capex and rising CWIP indicate the company is adding, not maxing out, capacity — an investment phase.
- CWIP trend: rising (₹84 Cr → ₹118 Cr → ₹141 Cr) — capex cycle live, commissioning over the next 1-2 years.

### Q1. Is incremental ROIC genuinely high (>20%) — and structural, not cyclical?

At the group level, no — and this is the honest answer the price ignores. Group ROCE is 10.5% (depressed) and even normalising the write-offs gets you to only ~14%. The high-return engine is *inside* Ceramics: a ₹1,268 Cr revenue segment earning ₹256 Cr of PBIT is running at a ~20% pre-tax segment margin and, on the modest capital that specialty-ceramics lines consume, an incremental ROIC comfortably above 20%. But the group blends that with a 4.3%-margin Abrasives business and capital that was destroyed overseas. So the correct statement is: the *segment* CUMI is reinvesting into earns >20%; the *group average* is mediocre because of legacy commodity and overseas capital. What would break even the Ceramics engine: Chinese specialty-ceramics and SiC oversupply reaching the engineered-ceramics tier, or the power-electronics ramp failing to materialise.

Math, group level (rough, using EBIT as NOPAT proxy pre-tax): FY26 operating profit ₹582 Cr on capital employed of roughly ₹4,300 Cr (reserves+equity+borrowings−investments) → ~13.5% pre-tax, ~10% post-tax ROCE. Ceramics segment: ₹256 Cr PBIT on a segment asset base that is a fraction of the group → the >20% figure. The mix is the whole argument.

### Q2. How much capital can be redeployed at these returns — the reinvestment runway?

The reinvestment runway is genuinely long *in Ceramics and advanced electrominerals*. Silicon-carbide-based power electronics for EVs and renewables is a global market growing double digits; India's semiconductor and defence localisation adds a domestic layer; and CUMI is one of very few integrated players (captive SiC grain → engineered ceramic). The ₹400 Cr FY27 capex is being pointed exactly here — advanced ceramics for power electronics, an integrated thermal-spray furnace, HPSiC/nitrides R&D. The constraint is not demand or capital (the group self-funds); it is (a) how fast CUMI can qualify into power-electronics supply chains, which takes years, and (b) that Ceramics is only ~24% of revenue, so even rapid segment growth moves the group slowly. Reinvestment rate is healthy — capex ₹309 Cr FY26 rising to ₹400 Cr FY27 against ₹300-320 Cr normalised PAT means the company reinvests essentially all of its earnings — but at a *blended* return closer to 14% than 20%. Runway: 7-10 years for Ceramics; the group compounds at the blend.

### Q3. What's the implied compounding math?

If the group reinvests ~90-100% of normalised earnings at a blended ~14% ROIC, sustainable growth ≈ 0.9 × 14% ≈ 12-13%. That is my base EPS CAGR. Layer on a one-time recovery bounce as FY26's ~₹134 Cr exceptional and the overseas operating losses drop out (reported EPS jumps from ₹10.22 back toward ₹16-18 in FY27 without any organic growth at all), and the near-term optics improve fast — but that is normalisation, not compounding, and the market has largely pre-paid for it. Durable compounding is the ~12-13% figure. For the stock to be a multi-bagger from here you would need the bull case — Ceramics/HPSiC becoming a third-plus of revenue and the market re-rating the group to a specialty-ceramics multiple — which is possible but not the base rate. These rates feed the Multi-Bagger Math table.

### Q4. What breaks the thesis?

**The compounding breaks if:** Ceramics growth stalls below high-single-digits (removing the mix-shift and the re-rating case) or the Russian VAW situation forces a further large impairment that also signals continued capital destruction.
**Leading indicator I'd see before the stock price tells me:** quarterly Ceramics *segment* revenue and PBIT — if segment growth decelerates and segment margin slips below ~18% for two-plus quarters, the engine is cooling before the headline shows it.

### Q5. Can I hold through a 40% drawdown? What's my conviction anchor?

**My conviction anchor (the sentence I re-read at −40%):**

**What I will NOT do in a drawdown:** I will not buy above ₹720 to "average in" — the whole discipline here is that quality does not fix price. Below ₹720, and especially toward the ₹640 add level and the ~₹500-520 floor, adding is rational because the asymmetry inverts in my favour. ## 6. Competitive Landscape

### Market Position

CUMI is the largest Indian abrasives manufacturer and a domestic leader in industrial ceramics, refractories and electrominerals, competing against Grindwell Norton (Saint-Gobain group) and Wendt India (in which CUMI itself holds a stake) domestically, and against global specialty players (3M, Saint-Gobain, Tyrolit, CoorsTek, Kyocera) and low-cost Chinese producers in electrominerals and engineered ceramics. Its differentiator is vertical integration — captive silicon-carbide and fused-alumina grain — and the breadth of doing all three verticals under one roof, which few peers match.

### Structural Advantages vs Peers

1. **Captive electrominerals (silicon-carbide grain):** CUMI makes its own SiC and fused alumina in electric-arc furnaces → competitors buying grain on the open market face cost and supply-security disadvantages, especially as Chinese export dynamics swing → underpins Ceramics/Abrasives cost position.
2. **Engineered-ceramics process IP + customer qualification:** decades of high-temperature sintering know-how and multi-year qualification into industrial/power-electronics supply chains → hard to replicate quickly → shows up as the ~20% Ceramics segment margin vs 4% Abrasives.
3. **Three-vertical breadth + Murugappa balance sheet:** net-cash, self-funding → can invest ₹400 Cr/year through a down-cycle when smaller players can't → captures the SiC/power-electronics capex race.

## Glossary — Key Industry Terms

**Abrasives**
Products that grind, cut, polish or finish other materials — bonded abrasives (grinding wheels), coated abrasives (sandpaper), and super abrasives (diamond/CBN wheels). CUMI's largest revenue segment but its lowest-margin (~4%), because much of it is commodity-like and sold on price against Grindwell Norton and imports.

**Ceramics (industrial/advanced ceramics)**
Engineered ceramic components — wear-resistant tiles/liners, metallised ceramics, and advanced ceramics for power electronics — made by sintering (fusing ceramic powder at very high temperature). Because they are specified to customer designs and hard to qualify, they command ~20% segment margins. This is CUMI's profit engine and the home of its growth optionality.

**Electrominerals**
The raw grain business: silicon carbide (SiC) and fused alumina made in energy-intensive electric-arc furnaces. CUMI uses this captively (feeding its own abrasives and ceramics) and sells it externally. Energy-cost-sensitive and exposed to Chinese oversupply — which is why its FY26 PBIT halved. The Russian VAW plant is an electrominerals asset.

**Silicon Carbide (SiC) / High-Purity SiC (HPSiC)**
A very hard, heat- and voltage-tolerant compound. As an abrasive grain it grinds hard metals; in high-purity form it is the basis for next-generation power semiconductors used in EV inverters and renewable-energy systems, which switch more efficiently than silicon. CUMI making its own SiC grain is the physical basis of its integration advantage and its power-electronics ambition.

**Volzhsky Abrasive Works (VAW)**
CUMI's Russian silicon-carbide/electrominerals subsidiary. The war-related sanctions environment has trapped ~₹297 Cr of its cash (can't be repatriated to India) and clouds the recoverability of its assets — the single biggest capital risk on CUMI's balance sheet.

**Foskor Zirconia / CUMI AWUKO**
Overseas subsidiaries — a zirconia (a ceramic mineral) unit in South Africa and an abrasives unit in Germany — that were chronically loss-making and are being voluntarily wound up, driving most of FY26's ~₹134 Cr exceptional charge.

**Refractories**
Heat-resistant materials (bricks, linings, castables) that line furnaces and kilns in steel, cement and glass making. Part of CUMI's Ceramics segment; the FY27 capex includes a new refractory tunnel kiln.

**PBIT (Profit Before Interest and Tax)**
Segment-level operating profit before financing and tax. Used here to compare how profitable each of CUMI's three segments is — the ~20% Ceramics PBIT margin vs ~4% Abrasives is the clearest single picture of where the company actually makes money.

**Standalone vs Consolidated**
Standalone = the parent Indian company only (and includes dividends it receives from subsidiaries). Consolidated = the parent plus all subsidiaries combined. For CUMI in FY26 the two diverge sharply (₹416 Cr standalone PAT vs ₹168 Cr consolidated) because the overseas subsidiaries lost money — consolidated is the correct valuation base.
