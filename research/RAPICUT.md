## Summary Verdict

### Why this business?

Rapicut Carbides has made tungsten carbide at Ankleshwar, Gujarat since 1979 — the hard, dense tips and inserts that do the actual cutting on a lathe tool, the drilling on a mining bit, the die that wire is drawn through. Tungsten carbide is roughly twice as stiff as steel and holds an edge at temperatures that would soften anything else, which is why it sits at the business end of almost every metal-cutting and rock-drilling operation on earth. India mines essentially none of the tungsten it needs; roughly 80% of world supply comes out of China.

That last fact is the entire story of the last eighteen months. China restricted tungsten exports in 2025, cut mining quotas, and in December 2025 announced that only fifteen firms would be licensed to export in 2026–27. January 2026 exports fell 40% year on year. Ammonium paratungstate — APT, the intermediate chemical everything downstream is made from — went from $1,650–1,900/MTU to above $3,100/MTU in Rotterdam, up more than 200% inside calendar 2026. Anyone holding tungsten inventory made a great deal of money.

Rapicut's numbers duly exploded: ₹10.38 Cr of quarterly revenue in June 2025 became ₹82.04 Cr in June 2026, and a ₹1.41 Cr loss became an ₹8.17 Cr profit. The stock is up 72% in three months and roughly 170% in a year, and closed the day the user screenshotted it locked at a single price of ₹341.40 — low equals high, which on a BSE-only microcap means it hit its circuit and no genuine two-way market existed.

But underneath the price move sits something that has nothing to do with tungsten. In mid-2025 the board approved absorbing United Wolfram Private Limited into Rapicut. UWPL is owned and directed by Abhishek Gami — who is also Rapicut's Chairman and Managing Director — and his wife Shruti. Rapicut will issue **3.31 crore fresh shares** to them. Rapicut currently has **0.54 crore shares outstanding**. That is the bet you are actually making, and it is not a bet on tungsten.

### The number that decides this

Everything turns on one ratio, so it is worth doing slowly.

Rapicut today has 53.86 lakh shares. Promoters hold 40.46% of them, so the public holds 59.54% — about 32.07 lakh shares. The merger adds 331 lakh new shares, all to the promoter family. The company then has 384.86 lakh shares, of which promoters hold 21.79 + 331 = 352.79 lakh, or 91.67%.

The public's 32.07 lakh shares don't change. But as a share of the company they fall from **59.54% to 8.33%**.

Put differently: for every ₹100 of the enlarged company's future earnings, existing outside shareholders have a claim on ₹8.33 where they used to have a claim on ₹59.54. Roughly **86% of your proportional ownership is transferred to the promoter family** in exchange for United Wolfram.

This is not necessarily theft. If United Wolfram is genuinely worth six times what listed Rapicut is worth, the ratio is fair and everyone ends up owning a smaller slice of a much bigger pie. That is exactly how a good merger is supposed to feel. The problem is that nobody outside the family can currently check.

Here is what is public about UWPL. CIN U28999GJ2022PTC137000 — incorporated in **2022**. Paid-up capital **₹1 lakh**. Revenue banded at **₹25–50 crore** on March 2024 numbers. Balance sheet last filed for the year ended 31 March 2023. Directors: Abhishek Vipinchandra Gami and Shruti Abhishek Gami. Plant at Mahuvej, Kosamba, near Surat. There is an ANI press release — paid distribution, not journalism — describing a new state-of-the-art tungsten plant and calling UWPL India's largest tungsten manufacturer.

A four-year-old private company with ₹1 lakh of equity capital and a last-known revenue band of ₹25–50 Cr is being exchanged for 86% of a listed company. It may well have grown enormously — a new plant plus a tripling in tungsten prices could plausibly do that. But the burden of proof sits with the party being issued the shares, and it has not yet been discharged in any document a shareholder can read.

That is not impossible, but nothing in the public record evidences it.

### The earnings are a price spike, not an engine

Set the merger aside entirely and Rapicut still fails the quality test, because its margins have no stable state.

| Quarter | Revenue (₹ Cr) | PAT (₹ Cr) | Net margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 FY26 (Jun-25) | 10.38 | (1.41) | loss |
| Q2 FY26 (Sep-25) | 15.82 | 1.25 | 7.9% |
| Q3 FY26 (Dec-25) | 20.78 | 1.57 | 7.6% |
| Q4 FY26 (Mar-26) | 49.31 | 0.65 | **1.3%** |
| Q1 FY27 (Jun-26) | 82.04 | 8.17 | **10.0%** |

Read the last two rows again. Revenue rose 66% and net margin went from 1.3% to 10.0%. One quarter earlier, revenue had risen 137% and margin had *collapsed* from 7.6% to 1.3%.

A manufacturer with real operating leverage does not behave like this. Operating leverage means the factory's fixed costs — salaries, rent, depreciation on the furnaces — stay flat while volume rises, so each extra rupee of sales drops disproportionately to profit and margins climb steadily as the plant fills. It produces a smooth, monotonic march upward in margin. What Rapicut shows instead is margin bouncing between 1.3% and 10% with no relationship to volume at all.

That signature belongs to a business whose profit is the gap between what it paid for tungsten and what it sold tungsten for. When you happen to be holding cheap inventory into a rising market, you print 10%. When you have to restock at the new price before you can reprice your output, you print 1.3%. The physical evidence supports this: Rapicut did ₹96 Cr of FY26 revenue on total assets of ₹65.5 Cr, and is now running at ₹328 Cr annualised. Turning your entire asset base five times a year is normal for a trading operation and physically implausible for a carbide plant, where the furnaces and presses are the constraint.

And the input that created the windfall is already turning. Chinese domestic APT sat at RMB 610,000/tonne in mid-August 2026 — **down 59.9% from its peak** and 9.0% since the start of the year. Western prices have stayed elevated because the export market has fragmented from the Chinese domestic one, so the squeeze isn't over. But the marginal direction has changed, and a business whose margin is an inventory position is exactly the business that gives back what the spike gave.

### Strengths

The operator is a genuine domain match, which the framework weights heavily and which I don't want to dismiss. Abhishek Gami didn't parachute in from private equity — he founded United Wolfram in 2011 as a tungsten and cobalt powder business, bought 27.77% of Rapicut in August 2019 for ₹6.26 Cr, spent five years in the industry, and took the CMD chair in 2025. On the Phase 4.4 test — strip out current results and ask whether you'd hire this person for exactly this job — the answer is yes. He has run tungsten powder before. The strategic logic of bolting upstream powder processing onto downstream carbide manufacturing is sound, and supply-chain integration is precisely what an Indian tungsten player should be doing while China is weaponising exports.

The balance sheet is clean and the audit is clean. FY26 borrowings of ₹6.71 Cr were repaid, leaving the company close to debt-free. K C Mehta & Co LLP signed the FY26 accounts with an **unmodified opinion** on 30 May 2026 — no qualification, no emphasis of matter reported. Operating cash flow came in at ₹11.77 Cr against ₹2.06 Cr of PAT, a cash conversion of 5.7x. That's the opposite of the classic Indian smallcap failure mode where profits are reported but cash never arrives, and it deserves credit even on a small base.

The end-market is real and structural. India consumes tungsten and mines none of it. Every tonne made domestically is import substitution against a supplier that has demonstrated willingness to use supply as leverage, and Western buyers are actively paying up to source outside China. Kennametal India — the listed comparable, making carbide tooling in Bengaluru — carries a ₹7,795 Cr market cap at roughly 39.8x earnings (17 Aug 2026), which tells you the market will pay a real multiple for a credible Indian carbide franchise.

### Concerns

**The merger is a related-party transaction covering 86% of the company, and that is a category of its own.** The framework's Phase 0.1 threshold flags related-party transactions above 5% of revenue. This isn't 5% of revenue — it is 86% of the equity, going to the person who chairs the board that approved it. The scheme may be perfectly fair. But a minority shareholder is being asked to accept a valuation set between the MD and himself, with the counterparty's audited accounts not in the public domain, and the fairness of it decided by a swap ratio fixed when the stock was a fraction of today's price. Phase 0.2's "planned equity dilution > 15% of the equity base" doesn't just fail here; it fails by a factor of forty.

**Post-merger promoter holding of 91.69% is illegal under SEBI's minimum public shareholding rule, which caps promoters at 75%.** This isn't a technicality — it means that within the mandated compliance window the promoter family must sell roughly 16.7% of the enlarged company into the market. On 384.86 lakh shares that's about **64 lakh shares** needing a buyer, against a current public float of 32 lakh shares. The forced supply is twice the entire existing float. Whatever price discovery looks like on the way there, it is not the price discovery of a stock with 32 lakh shares and a circuit filter.

**The earnings driver is a foreign government's policy lever, and the framework is explicit that this disqualifies.** Phase 0.3 flags "revenue dependent on one commodity or reversible regulatory tailwind." Rapicut is dependent on one commodity *and* on a reversible regulatory action, and the regulator in question is Beijing. China can loosen quotas by decree; the mechanism that tripled APT can be untripled the same way, and the domestic Chinese price rolling over 59.9% from peak suggests the process has started.

**Liquidity makes this uninvestable at size regardless of the thesis.** The screenshot shows today's low and today's high both at ₹341.40 — the stock traded at exactly one price all day, which on the BSE means it opened at its circuit limit and stayed there. It is BSE-only, the free float is ~32 lakh shares, and it has moved 170% in a year. An investor who wants out of a position of any meaningful size on a bad day will find there is no bid, because circuit filters work in both directions.

The scenarios below are built on the **post-merger share count of 384.86 lakh**, because that is what a buyer at ₹341.40 will own. Building them on today's 53.86 lakh shares would produce the flattering numbers that make this stock look cheap, and would be wrong.

```
         ₹2,700 Cr / 3.8486 Cr shares = ₹702/share
```

| | Value | vs CMP ₹341.40 |
|---|---|---|
| Bear | ₹95 | **−72%** |
| **Base** | **₹229** | **−33%** |
| Bull | ₹702 | +106% |

```
Margin of Safety = (Base − CMP) / Base = (229 − 341.40) / 229 = −49%

The stock trades 49% ABOVE base-case fair value. The margin of safety is
not thin. It is negative — you are paying a premium to your own best estimate.

Downside if wrong = (341.40 − 95) / 341.40 = 72%
Upside to base     = negative
Upside to bull     = +106%

Asymmetry ratio (bull ÷ bear, the most generous possible framing) = 106 / 72 = 1.5x
```

The framework requires an asymmetry ratio above 2x to buy anything and treats anything below 1x as an automatic no. Even measuring the *bull* case against the bear — which flatters the stock by comparing the best outcome to the risk, rather than the expected outcome to the risk — this gets to 1.5x. Measured properly, base against bear, the expected return from here is negative. A Grade D business requires "do not buy at any price"; a Grade D business with a negative margin of safety doesn't need the grade to reach the same answer.

### The Compounding Equation

There isn't one, and that is the finding rather than a failure to find it.

A compounding engine needs profits earned on capital deployed, ploughed back into more capital that earns the same return, over a long runway. Rapicut has produced one exceptional quarter whose profit came from the difference between an old purchase price and a new selling price for a metal it does not mine. You cannot reinvest a price spike. When APT goes back down, the capital deployed into inventory at $3,100/MTU earns a negative return, and the same mechanism that generated ₹8.17 Cr generates a writedown.

Run the Phase 4.5.1 five-whys and it terminates outside the company in three steps rather than five:

1. Why does Rapicut earn a 12.7% PBT margin today? Because tungsten sold for more than the tungsten it had bought.
2. Why? Because APT tripled inside twelve months.
3. Why? Because China restricted exports and cut quotas, and China is ~80% of world supply.
4. Why? Because Beijing is using critical-minerals supply as trade leverage.
5. Why? Geopolitics.

The framework's test is whether the fifth answer points to something structural — a network, a licence, a geographic monopoly, a switching cost buried in a customer's plant — or to something the company merely happens to be standing near. This one lands on a foreign policy decision that can be reversed by announcement, and is already partially reversing. That is the definition of a fragile moat.

The genuinely durable version of this business does exist and is worth naming: an integrated Indian tungsten player, owning the chemistry from ore to powder to sintered carbide, serving domestic customers who structurally cannot buy Chinese any more. That business would compound. Whether Rapicut-plus-United-Wolfram *is* that business is precisely the question the scheme document would answer and currently doesn't.

### Second-Order Stress Test

**Two years out (FY28), if the base case plays out.** The scheme has cleared NCLT, the combined entity is doing perhaps ₹700 Cr of revenue at a mid-single-digit net margin, and the tungsten price has settled well below the 2026 spike. Management's agenda is dominated by two things it has never done: integrating and reporting a merged entity to listed-company standards, and executing a forced sell-down from 91.69% to 75% under SEBI's minimum public shareholding rule. That sell-down is the defining event of the period — roughly 64 lakh shares against a 32 lakh float — and it will set the price far more than the P&L will.

**Five years out.** Two paths, and they diverge on a question that is answerable today but hasn't been answered. If United Wolfram's plant is real and at scale, the combined company is a legitimate integrated Indian tungsten champion at a moment when the entire Western supply chain wants a non-Chinese source, and it earns a Kennametal-style multiple. If UWPL is closer to its last filed size, the merged entity is a ₹300–400 Cr revenue commodity converter carrying a share count sized for something much larger, and the per-share arithmetic never recovers.

**What success itself creates.** A tungsten business that visibly makes money at these margins invites two responses. Established players — Kennametal, Sandvik, the Chinese majors themselves once controls ease — have the balance sheets to price aggressively into an Indian market that has just proved it will pay. And a promoter sitting at 91.69% who has watched his stock go up 170% has every incentive to satisfy the MPS requirement at the top rather than the bottom, which puts his selling interest and the minority's holding interest in direct opposition at exactly the moment the story is most exciting.

**Margin behaviour through stress (Phase 4.5.3).** The framework asks for one of three acceptable states: margins stable through the stress, recovering from an identified one-time, or absorbed by capex with prior-cycle proof. Rapicut fits none. Its margins over five quarters went loss → 7.9% → 7.6% → 1.3% → 10.0%, with no identified cause for the swings other than commodity timing and no prior capex cycle to point at. The framework calls this a disqualifier even at low valuation, and this valuation is not low.

**Market label vs reality (Phase 4.5.4).**
- *Market's label:* "India's tungsten play — cheap at 16x, earnings exploding, riding the China shortage."
- *My label:* "A pre-merger shell whose quoted multiple is an artifact of a share count about to rise 7.1x, earning inventory gains on a commodity that has already peaked, with 86% of the post-merger value sitting in a private company nobody outside the promoter's family has seen audited."
- *Label-change catalyst:* publication of the scheme document with UWPL's audited FY25–FY26 financials and the registered valuer's report. That single document resolves this in either direction — and until it exists, no amount of further analysis substitutes for it.

### Pre-existing capability check (Phase 0.5)

Rapicut's Ankleshwar plant is real, has been operating since 1979, and qualifies as "built and operating." But it generated ₹42 Cr of revenue and a ₹2.33 Cr loss in FY25 — this is not the asset the thesis rests on.

The asset the thesis rests on is United Wolfram's Mahuvej facility, which is Form 3 (acquired and integrated) on paper but has not been legally integrated, has not been independently verified, and belongs to a company whose last public balance sheet is for FY23. The framework's instruction for a thesis resting on something not yet built or not yet verifiable is to cap the position at 1–2% and treat it as venture-grade. Combined with a Grade D score and a negative margin of safety, that reduces to zero.

### When would I re-underwrite?

This is an AVOID with named, observable conditions rather than a permanent dismissal. The business could become genuinely interesting. Three things would have to happen:

1. **The scheme document is published with UWPL's audited FY25 and FY26 financials and the registered valuer's report.** If UWPL turns out to be running at ₹300 Cr+ of revenue with ₹30 Cr+ of PAT, the 3.31 crore share issuance is defensible and the combined entity at ₹1,314 Cr is arguable. # Detailed Analysis

## 1. Business Summary

Rapicut Carbides Limited (CIN L28910GJ1977PLC002998) was incorporated in April 1977 and began commercial production of tungsten carbide products in October 1979 at Ankleshwar, Gujarat. Gujarat Drillwell Private Limited, promoted with Rapicut's technical knowhow, merged into the company in 1993.

The product range covers tungsten carbide tips and inserts, wire-drawing nibs, bar and tube drawing pellets, carbide rings and flats, jute eyelets and solid carbide cutters, alongside powder products — tungsten metal powder, tungsten carbide powder, fused tungsten carbide and ready-to-press powders. End markets are metal cutting, mining, wear parts and general engineering.

What tungsten carbide actually is, in plain terms: tungsten metal chemically bonded to carbon, then mixed with cobalt as a binder and sintered under heat and pressure into a solid. The result is roughly twice as stiff as steel and keeps its hardness at temperatures where steel tools would soften and deform. That is why it forms the cutting edge of lathe tools, the button on a mining drill bit, and the die through which wire is pulled down to gauge. It wears out and gets replaced, which makes it a consumable — good for recurring demand, but also a product where the buyer is price-sensitive and switching between suppliers is straightforward.

India has essentially no domestic tungsten mining. Roughly 80% of global supply originates in China. Every Indian carbide manufacturer therefore starts from imported ore or imported APT, which makes the entire domestic industry a converter of an imported commodity — and makes input cost, not manufacturing skill, the dominant swing factor in any given quarter's profit.

Control changed hands in August 2019, when Abhishek Gami and Shruti Gami signed a share purchase agreement for 27.77% from a group of sellers for ₹6.26 Cr (Abhishek 1.34 million shares / 25%, Shruti 0.15 million / 2.77%), followed by the mandatory open offer for a further 26%. Abhishek Gami became Chairman & Managing Director in 2025. He founded United Wolfram in 2011 and has directed United Wolfram Pvt Ltd since 2021.

## 3. Compounding Engine — Q&A

**Q0. Operating Leverage Test — does growth create margin leverage?**

No, and the data is unambiguous. Q4 FY26 grew revenue 137% QoQ and net margin *fell* from 7.6% to 1.3%. Q1 FY27 grew revenue 66% QoQ and net margin *rose* from 1.3% to 10.0%. The framework's formula — OPM change in bps divided by revenue growth % — is not just negative, it changes sign quarter to quarter, which means the relationship being measured doesn't exist. Margin here is set by the spread between tungsten purchase and sale prices, not by absorbing fixed costs over volume.

**Q1. Is incremental ROIC genuinely high (>20%) — and structural, not cyclical?**

Not computable with any honesty, and misleading if forced. The three-year delta the framework asks for spans FY23–FY26, a period in which the company was loss-making at the start and in which the business model appears to have changed. A naive calculation using FY26 EBIT against a ₹65.5 Cr asset base would produce a flattering number that reflects a commodity price move rather than capital productivity. The framework's Phase 1 instruction is to show the math; the honest output here is that the denominator and numerator are measuring different businesses.

What can be said: the company did ₹96 Cr of FY26 revenue on ₹65.5 Cr of total assets and is annualising ₹328 Cr on a similar base. Five turns of the asset base per year is a trading characteristic. Carbide sintering is capital-intensive and slow — furnaces and presses are the constraint, and you cannot run them five times faster because tungsten got expensive.

**Q2. How much capital can be redeployed at these returns — the reinvestment runway?**

Unknown, and the question is premature. Reinvestment rate requires capex, depreciation and working-capital change, none of which are available in this session. More fundamentally, the returns being earned are inventory gains, and inventory gains are not a return on capital that can be compounded — they are a one-time transfer from whoever sold tungsten cheap to whoever held it while the price rose.

**Q3. What's the implied compounding math?**

There is no defensible g = reinvestment rate × ROIC calculation here, because ROIC is a commodity artifact and the share count is about to increase 7.1x. The relevant math is the dilution arithmetic in the Summary Verdict, which is the only compounding happening — and it compounds against the minority shareholder.

**Q4. What breaks the thesis?**

There is no long thesis to break. What would break the *avoid* is documented evidence that United Wolfram is a substantial operating business and that Rapicut's margin survives a normalised tungsten price. Both are testable and neither has been tested.

**Q5. Can I hold through a 40% drawdown?**

The question presumes a position. Note that a 40% drawdown from ₹341.40 lands at ₹205 — still 4% *above* the mid-May all-time high, and still 44% above bear case. The drawdown risk here is not 40%; the bear case is −72%, and circuit filters mean it would be reached in a sequence of sessions where selling is not possible.

## Glossary

**APT (Ammonium Paratungstate)** — the white crystalline chemical intermediate that almost all tungsten products are made from. Ore is converted to APT, APT to tungsten oxide, oxide to tungsten metal powder, and powder to tungsten carbide. Priced in MTU (metric tonne units) and quoted on Rotterdam and Chinese benchmarks. Because India mines no tungsten, APT is the input cost that sets Indian carbide profitability.

**Tungsten carbide** — tungsten chemically bonded with carbon, mixed with cobalt binder and sintered under heat and pressure. Roughly twice as stiff as steel and retains hardness at temperatures that soften steel. Used wherever something must cut, drill or resist wear.

**Minimum Public Shareholding (MPS)** — SEBI's requirement that promoters of a listed company hold no more than 75%, ensuring a real public float. Post-merger promoter holding of 91.69% breaches this and must be corrected within the mandated window, forcing promoter sales into the market.

**Circuit filter** — an exchange-imposed daily price band on a stock. When a stock is "circuit-locked," it has hit the band and trades at only that one price, with orders queued on one side. It means the day's price is not the outcome of two-sided trading, and that exits may be unavailable when they are most wanted.

**Swap ratio** — in a merger settled in shares rather than cash, the number of acquirer shares issued per target share. Fixed in share terms, so the resulting ownership split does not change if the market price moves.
